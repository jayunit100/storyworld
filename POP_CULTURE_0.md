# This is V0 of our Canonized Pop Culture Story

Pop culture weaves together music, film, comics, and games into the tapestry of history. 
Each decade’s art is shaped by political movements, economic realities, and societal shifts.

From the hard-hitting protest songs of the ‘70s to the neon-splashed metal mania and gritty comics of the ‘80s—and onward to the boundary-pushing alternative rock and hip-hop surges of the ‘90s—pop culture reflects the rebellion, innovation, and storytelling that resonate across generations.

In the 70s we invented the modern power chord.
We perfected it in the 80s, and made it progressive.
And in the 90s, the breadth of available music and high quality, commited fans peaked.

We aim to bring back the era of deep commitment to pop culture in america : mostly with regard to comic books, and rock n roll.

## The 1970s: Rebellion & Escapism

By the early ‘70s, Led Zeppelin, The Rolling Stones, and Pink Floyd were channeling cultural unrest into masterful, lengthy rock albums.

Meanwhile: 
- the Sex Pistols and The Clash jumpstarted a punk movement, railing against economic decline and government indifference. 
- As the Vietnam War weighed on public consciousness, protest songs like “Fortunate Son” (Creedence Clearwater Revival) and “War” (Edwin Starr) captured anti-war fervor.
- In the Bronx, a transformative music culture emerged when DJs—led by Kool Herc—started looping breaks, prompting MCs to rap about urban realities. 

Across town, clubs lit up to carefree disco tunes by The Bee Gees, Donna Summer, and Chic, who delivered escapism via flashing lights and dance floors. At the movies, Star Wars (1977) introduced the Force and lightsabers, while Alien (1979) made “space horror” a new genre standard.
Superhero comics also became more socially aware. Green Lantern/Green Arrow tackled drug addiction and systemic injustice, and X-Men (especially in how it handled mutant outcasts) emerged as an allegory for civil rights. Video games gained momentum with Pong (1972), the first widely popular game, followed by Space Invaders (1978), which conquered arcades. And although men often hogged the headlines, women made waves, too: Stevie Nicks, lead vocalist for Fleetwood Mac, captivated fans on albums like Rumours (1977).

## The 1980s: MTV, Metal, & Darker Comics

- The ‘80s exploded with MTV, turning songs into visual spectacles. 
- Michael Jackson’s “Thriller”, Madonna (famed for Like a Virgin), and Prince (breaking barriers with Purple Rain) dominated a music-video revolution. 

On the heavier side, 
- Guns N’ Roses, Bon Jovi, and Def Leppard fueled “hair metal”.
- A darker and less flashy style of "thrash metal" stormed in with the “Big Four”:
  - Metallica, 
  - Megadeth, 
  - Slayer,
  - Anthrax. 

One of those thrash titans, Metallica, scored one of history’s best-selling heavy albums with their self-titled Black Album.
At the same time, rap became mainstream thanks to groups like Run-D.M.C. and Public Enemy, who eventually teamed with Anthrax to blend rap and rock on “Bring the Noise.” Comics grew grimmer with works like Batman: The Dark Knight Returns, offering a dystopian take on the Caped Crusader, and Watchmen, illustrated by Dave Gibbons, which dissected the very notion of heroism.
Children’s pop culture thrived: My Little Pony delighted kids with pastel-colored adventures, and Pee-wee’s Playhouse, hosted by Paul Reubens, offered surreal humor. Sesame Street, launched in 1969, continued educating youngsters with letters and numbers. Meanwhile, the big screen introduced the unstoppable cyborg of The Terminator (1984), played by Arnold Schwarzenegger. Traditional rock bands flourished too: AC/DC blasted forward with Back in Black, and Queen’s hits remained undeniable. In video games, the Atari 2600 was the first commercially successful home console, setting the stage for Nintendo’s NES to dominate with Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, and Metroid.
Pop’s female voices grew more powerful. Janet Jackson shook the industry with her album Rhythm Nation, Cyndi Lauper declared “Girls Just Wanna Have Fun,” and Paula Abdul—a onetime Laker Girl and choreographer—topped charts with “Straight Up.” This era also saw new wave and synthpop from Duran Duran, Depeche Mode, and Pet Shop Boys, revealing futuristic sonic landscapes. 
We love British Pop.  Sometimes we wonder how such a small country made so many amazing bands.

## The 1990s: Grunge, Alternative, & Pop-Punk

Deeply technical metal and hard rock were disrupted in the 1990s by Seattle's rainy grunge scene.  This stew of counter-culture gave birth to Pearl Jam, fronted by Eddie Vedder, Soundgarden, Nirvana, and Alice in Chains, all forging a raw, angsty “grunge” aesthetic. Meanwhile, pop-punk arrived in full force with Green Day and The Offspring, who brought youthful rebellion to mainstream radio.

Women continued shaping the music scene. 4 Non Blondes, led by Linda Perry, scored a massive hit with “What’s Up?”. 
Hole, fronted by Courtney Love, blended grunge guitars with a visceral female perspective. 

And just a generation earlier, ex-Runaway Lita Ford had cracked the ‘80s rock charts, proving female rockers could shred, too.

On the opposite side of the musical spectrum, the later 90s saw a revival of high-quality british and american, progressive pop bands. 

- The Spice Girls ruled global pop, championing “Girl Power” with hits like “Wannabe” and “Say You’ll Be There.”   
- The new wave of British pop overshadowed the gloom of grunge. 

Meanwhile,  fellow Brits Oasis dropped “Wonderwall,” a defining anthem of ‘90s Britpop.

Hip-hop and R&B soared to massive popularity behind artists like Tupac, Notorious B.I.G., Wu-Tang Clan, Mariah Carey, and Whitney Houston. 

Simultaneously: Comics rode a colossal wave. 

- The newly formed Image Comics presented edgy fare like Spawn, created by Todd McFarlane—one of the era’s top antiheroes.
- DC’s “Death of Superman” storyline made national headlines, and we stood in lines for hours to get a hold of it.

In animation, Toy Story (1995) introduced a cowboy and space ranger in a fully computer-generated adventure. Disney also produced its first major ‘90s feature with a female protagonist disguised as a soldier: Mulan, an empowering saga. Gamers embraced the leap to 3D with the Sony PlayStation and Nintendo 64, indulging in groundbreaking titles like Final Fantasy VII and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time. Arcades still thrived with iconic staples: Pac-Man (that dot-gobbling yellow hero) had already become a classic in the ‘80s, but it endured well into the ‘90s.

## Yacht and Psychadelic Rock

Although not mentioned above - we can't forget about the softer rock bands which defined pop culture in the late 60s and 70s.

Yacht Rock emerged in the 70s as a soft rock genre that peaked around the early 1980s.   It features polished production.   Doobie Brothers, Steely Dan, Christopher Cross, Kenny Loggins, Toto, and Hall & Oates all personified it.
Steely Dan is still considered to be one of the most important bands in the history of popular American rock - elevating the genre's critical acclaim significantly.

StoryWorld considers Procol Harum ~ the creators of the epic "A Whiter Shade of Pale" (in the 60's) ~ another yacht rock band, although other's might put them in the category of psychadelic rock.

## Rap-Rock Fusion & Internet Culture’s Downside

Shortly before the millennium, “rap-rock” gained traction. Aerosmith and Run-D.M.C. famously collaborated on “Walk This Way,” and the Beastie Boys deftly infused rap attitudes with rock grit. Soon, “nu metal” arrived via Korn, Limp Bizkit, and Linkin Park, mixing down-tuned riffs with rapped verses. Rage Against the Machine roared with political outcries, fueled by Tom Morello’s inventive guitar wizardry.
Yet as the internet age dawned, music experienced fragmentation. Cheap downloads and social media “click-and-skip” culture frayed once-cohesive fan communities. Casual listeners hopped from track to track, neglecting the deep bond once formed by collecting albums, reading liner notes, and following entire tours. The rapid pace diluted the communal energy once essential to music scenes.

## The Post Cell Phone Apocalypse & The Return of Real Bands

Despite digital oversaturation, a new wave of music culture is brewing—one that values real instruments, basement jam sessions, and full-length conceptual storytelling. In this potential future, smartphones lose their grip, and earnest fans reignite the social currency of local shows, dedicated fandom, and the raw power of a live guitar amp. This renewed fervor, echoing the rebellious spirit behind punk, metal, grunge, and rap-rock, could spark a new golden age of band-driven pop culture—honest, unfiltered, and forging communities that resonate deeply across generations.
Emerging rock bands that are bring the raw energy of real music back to the USA:
- Greta Van Fleet – Known for their throwback rock sound, soaring vocals, and emphasis on live performance.
- King Gizzard & The Lizard Wizard – Prolific, genre-bending psych-rockers who keep the DIY spirit alive with numerous albums and energetic shows.
- IDLES – A raw, punk-infused band that champions honest lyrics, cathartic riffs, and an unfiltered live experience.
- Turnstile – Fusing hardcore’s intensity with melodic hooks, they’ve built a loyal fan base through passionate performances.
- Black Midi – Experimental rock musicians pushing boundaries with complex compositions and improvisational flair in their live sets.
